Volunteer pilots and aircraft for California's emergencies

By the duty coordinator, Livermore · 15 DARTs · 240 members · 96 aircraft · 11 counties with a signed agreement

Photo: Cessna 182 being loaded at Livermore

On the ramp at Livermore. Twelve aircraft from six DARTs flew the spring exercise, moving 4,200 lb of supplies to San Luis Obispo in a single day.

CalDART organizes pilots, aircraft owners and ground crews into local teams at general aviation airports around the state. When an earthquake, wildfire or flood closes the roads, those teams fly relief supplies, responders and information to the communities that need them.

A DART is a Disaster Airlift Response Team: a group of pilots, aircraft owners and ground volunteers based at one general aviation airport and led by a DART coordinator. Fifteen of them run from Santa Rosa to Santa Monica, grouped into the North Bay, the East Bay, the Peninsula, the South Bay, the Central Coast and Southern California.

CalDART gives California's emergency managers a trained, insured and practiced volunteer air transportation capability. Teams are organized, exercised and kept current all year, so they are ready on the day an agency calls. Three activations and six exercises went on the books in the past twelve months.

Who joins. Pilots hold a private pilot certificate or higher, a current medical or BasicMed, and access to an aircraft; flight time counts toward currency. Aircraft owners bring single-engine and light twin aircraft based at a member airport. Ground crew need no pilot certificate at all — the work is loading, radio, logistics and airport liaison — and every team wants more of it.

Emergency managers — county OES staff, hospitals, tribal governments and mutual-aid partners — request support through the duty coordinator at any hour. Eleven counties have a signed agreement on file.

Membership runs $45 a year, renewable each year, or $650 once for life. Pilots without an aircraft are paired with owners in their own DART, so a certificate and a free Saturday are enough to start.

How an activation works

From the county's telephone call to the logged leg — the sequence every mission follows

Local teams form around an airport. Each DART is a group of pilots, aircraft owners and ground volunteers based at one general aviation airport and led by a DART coordinator.

An agency requests air support. A county emergency operations center or a partner agency contacts CalDART's duty coordinator with the mission: what, where and by when.

The coordinator matches the mission to aircraft. Available aircraft and pilots are matched to the load, the distance and conditions at the destination airport.

Volunteers fly the mission. Pilots fly at their own discretion and expense under standard FAA rules. Ground crews load, unload and hand off the cargo.

Every flight is logged. Each leg is recorded for the agency's records and for CalDART's own after-action review.

Briefs

Livermore, 14 April 2026. Spring exercise moves 4,200 lb of supplies between Livermore and San Luis Obispo. Twelve aircraft from six DARTs flew a simulated post-earthquake resupply, with the Central Coast DART receiving and staging every load.

Big Sur, 2 March 2026. Winter storm activation: three flights for Monterey County OES. Pilots from Watsonville and Reid-Hillview carried water-testing kits and two public health staff to Big Sur after Highway 1 closed.

San Jose, 19 February 2026. Annual meeting set for 7 June at Reid-Hillview. Elections, the treasurer's report and a briefing from Santa Clara County OES. All members are welcome; coordinators from the fifteen DARTs are expected.
